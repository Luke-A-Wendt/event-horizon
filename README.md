## Object families

Black holes, stars, planets, ships, and astronauts can have children. Children inherit their parent’s motion and add their own local orbit. The placement hierarchy is black hole → star → planet → moon → spacecraft → astronaut. An equal-class pair is allowed, while comparable-mass triples and chains are rejected. Compact children with at most one tenth of their parent’s mass may form deeper, branching hierarchies. This is a composition rule, not a universal physical mass ordering. Paired bodies use prescribed barycentric circular motion; nested systems are not a numerical many-body stability solver.

Accretion discs and jets attach to black holes or stars; protostars and compact stars can accrete and launch outflows without an event horizon. Rings and asteroid belts attach to stars or planets and offer icy, rocky, and metallic appearances. These attachments are leaf nodes and cannot have satellites. Deleting a parent through the tree removes its whole branch. Reparenting rejects cycles and incompatible hosts. The logical scene supports up to 1,024 bodies and attachments, alongside the primary/binary black-hole rendering path. Additional compact bodies can form mass-separated hierarchies; children of at most one tenth their parent’s mass can share an orbital level, while comparable compact peers remain pair-limited. A bounded set of two to twelve relevant bodies, depending on scene size and GPU uniform capacity, is selected for detailed shading each frame; the catalog and object cameras retain the full hierarchy. Moons are a smaller class than planets, so a planet can have many moons. Pair restrictions apply to comparable compact peers and other equal-class objects.

Stars have editable class, color, radius, mass, angular velocity, spots, and corona. Classes include main-sequence stars, red dwarfs, protostars, red giants, supergiants, white dwarfs, neutron stars, and hypothetical Thorne–Żytkow objects. Stellar angular velocity Ω is in radians per second; a* is derived from an approximate moment of inertia and is not the same control. Mass and radius show the model’s compactness threshold. Crossing it changes the same object into a compact-horizon representation without losing its identity or children. This threshold is a visualization criterion, not a universal stability calculation: real collapse depends on pressure, equation of state, rotation, mass loss, and the full spacetime. **The point of no return** cycles radius through the threshold; its expanding half is explicitly a fictional reset. Near a black hole, a procedural tidal stream responds to distance, mass, and spin: zero spin removes its angular swirl. It illustrates stripped plasma rather than solving stellar magnetohydrodynamics. Planets offer rocky, ocean, gas, and ice appearances. Named worlds additionally offer reference surface maps, distinct atmospheric features, and approximate triaxial shapes for irregular bodies. The starfield uses independent randomized layers with brightness and color-diversity controls.

Select **Track**, **Ride · 360°**, or **Free roam here** from an object's properties. NASA Shuttle and Starship Enterprise expose **Drive** in their properties, Camera, Map, and beside the viewport status. Drive switches to Orbit and tracks the selected ship: the Enterprise from the side, traveling left to right, and the Shuttle from behind. Warp changes fit the camera to the current bubble; at warp zero it frames the ship with clear margins around the controls. The main **Camera** panel offers Orbit, Chase and Ride viewpoints; Enterprise and Shuttle wormhole travelers initially use **Orbit** from the same normalized position behind the vehicle. Corner steering buttons choose headings in 5° increments; holding a button repeats the steps. Manual Enterprise flight uses a compact Warp slider. The Shuttle retains its forward-acceleration slider. Speed, angular velocity, and eligible object clocks appear beneath the object distance, with two significant figures. **Stop motion** slows the Enterprise; **Stop** smoothly reduces the Shuttle’s thrust to zero while it coasts. **Exit drive (×)** blends back to the assigned parent-relative orbit, a prescribed navigation transition rather than a modeled engine burn.

**Opacity** is available in the properties of stars, planets, moons, probes, and spacecraft. Translucency reveals objects behind a surface without changing its mass or physical dimensions. Geometry mode uses opaque gray bodies.

The normal 3D view uses physical reference sizes or explicitly labeled proxies for unmeasured bodies. Orbit map uses equal-size schematic symbols. Locator labels remain selectable for distant catalog bodies, with stable text placement and anchors from the displayed optical chart. GPU labels follow the approximate lens model; the CPU preview remains schematic.

The bridge is a distinct **Wormholes** scene family, not an orbitable body. Its default has a plain starfield on one side and a purple nebula on the other. Its coordinate sheet defaults to **Perpendicular**, normal to the traveler’s initial direction; **Parallel** is a separate choice. In **Shadow through the throat**, the black-hole grid stays in the flight plane. ### Save and load scenes

Open **Scenes → Save scene** to download a human-readable `.yaml` file. **Load scene** opens a saved file from your device. Objects are nested beneath their parents, alongside editable properties; the file also stores the environment, display settings, simulation clock, and viewpoint. Loading opens the saved scene paused and retains the current GPU or CPU backend.

Scene files require an exact application-version match: **1.38.0**. A different version shows **Cannot load** and leaves the current scene unchanged. Saving during a guided transfer preserves the current position and clocks; loading resumes manual control with guidance cancelled. The reader accepts the generated YAML structure with ordinary scalar edits and validates object relationships, with a 4 MB file limit. Formatting and validation run in a local worker; browsers without worker support can handle only small scenes and files.

### Persistent object clocks

Astronaut, Shuttle and ISS share a distance-based gravitational comparison and a proper-acceleration clock. **Rate** is `dt/dτ`; **Time dilation** is the accumulated clock difference `t − τ = ∫(dt/dτ − 1) dτ`, starting at creation. During ordinary playback and manual flight, active wall-clock seconds supply onboard time `τ`, independently of playback speed. Guided transfers instead advance the ship’s clocks through their estimated physical journey time while the visual trip is compressed. An initially stationary traveler in empty space has a rate of 1× and zero accumulated difference. Both readouts use two significant figures, with readable duration units and a compact rate multiplier. Their clocks persist through Drive, Exit drive and view changes. Pausing, loading, hidden tabs and Picture studio freeze accumulation. The Enterprise is exempt.

The gravitational comparison is stationary Schwarzschild outside a nonrotating mass. A horizon-relative radial shift provides an explicitly approximate comparison for spinning holes. A finite body-size clearance keeps eligible objects outside the horizon. Constant proper acceleration from rest gives `dt/dτ = cosh(aτ/c)`; changing headings use an approximate vector-rapidity update. Prescribed orbital speed is not added to this simplified model, and clock values do not alter scene dynamics.

### Steering and the Alcubierre comparison

Speed and angular-velocity readouts describe the resulting motion. Enterprise speed is expressed in multiples of c, and Shuttle speed in meters per second. The Enterprise field follows its center trajectory and travel frame. At warp zero, the warp contribution to optical bending and glow vanishes. Constant-time spatial slices are flat even when four-dimensional spacetime is curved. The visible bubble, neighboring-body composition, and path preview are finite diagnostic effects, not an exact accelerating warp metric or a solved combined black-hole/warp spacetime. Manual Enterprise flight always uses Warp, including within a wormhole. Automatic wormhole travel remains a prescribed impulse crossing. The Enterprise’s red warp range marks emergency or overstress operation from warp 8, with episode-dependent limits. The NASA Shuttle starts Drive with zero velocity and zero thrust. Its forward acceleration control spans 0–10g in half-g steps, with g = 9.80665 m/s². Cutting thrust preserves inertial velocity. Turning changes the vehicle’s heading; a forward burn after a 180° turn reduces the original velocity. Thrust is proper acceleration in a planar special-relativistic model: coordinate speed stays below c, and coordinate acceleration falls as the Lorentz factor grows. The two clock readouts show the current reference/onboard rate and accumulated clock difference, using active wall time as the onboard input. They persist through Drive, Exit drive and view resets, and use readable units with two significant figures. Numerical precision is not a claim of physical accuracy. The Enterprise is exempt from this clock comparison, including its automatic crossings. The slider marks 4–10g red as a conservative high-load warning, not a universal survival boundary; tolerance depends on duration, orientation, support, and physiology. This is not a specification of the real Shuttle’s engines. See [NASA occupant protection](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-024-acceleration.pdf).

In Drive, **Labels** highlights valid orbital destinations. Tap a label, or select a destination in **Map**, to review the estimated trip time and choose **Go**. Map retains its object hierarchy, orbit editing and camera tools; the flight preview stays beside the selected destination. Every guided journey compresses its modeled duration into ten active viewing seconds, smoothly turns and accelerates the vehicle, and ends in an orbit around the destination. A stabilized camera follows the craft through its braking turn and brings the destination into the arrival view. Shuttle estimates use an ideal 1g accelerating and braking transfer with relativistic onboard and reference times; the lifetime clock includes the trip’s time difference. Enterprise transfers use Warp 1 and remain exempt from dilation. Estimates use the current separation and an approximate gravity correction, rather than a full trajectory or fuel calculation. Arrival orbits stay at least twice the target’s physical radius away from its center, with extra clearance for compact objects. **Cancel** returns control at the current position.

## Performance behavior

Adaptive targets 15–30 completed frames per second. It starts at Low, steps down below 15 FPS, and tests the next tier after sustained headroom above 30 FPS. At Low, it can reduce pixel resolution further and restore it as performance recovers; the scene’s object-slot limit stays unchanged. Low, Medium, and High use progressively larger sampling budgets, while Ultra remains a manual option. The status readout shows the selected mode, active tier, frame rate, and GPU ON or GPU OFF.

The full Solar System catalog remains available to the tree, map, labels, and cameras. Large authored catalogs retain validated templates in memory for subsequent scene loads; each visit creates independent objects, so scene edits do not change the template. Detailed shading is limited to relevant visible bodies. Negligible weak-field contributions are omitted from costly light-ray integration, while physical masses and prescribed orbital motion remain intact. Close-up surface detail is concentrated on the inspected world; distant subpixel bodies do not receive the same expensive surface work.

Where supported, a local Worker and OffscreenCanvas separate rendering from the interface. Camera updates replace older pending updates, GPU submissions use nonblocking fences, and the CPU preview yields between drawing batches. Scene loading shows the scene name, an animated symbol, and elapsed time as a percentage of the 60-second loading limit. A separate small 2D worker draws the indicator where supported; older browsers use prepainted compositor animations, including a startup indicator visible before the application bundle executes. A retry action appears when rendering fails. Browsers without worker WebGL use a main-thread GPU fallback, so driver stalls cannot be fully isolated on every device.

Geometry mode renders shaded gray stars and planets along the same optical rays as the scene, including lensing and foreground occlusion. The surrounding grid remains a coordinate diagnostic within the approximate optical model. The compact-star orbital study slows its simulation clock to show a circuit in roughly 30 seconds, without changing the modeled orbit size or mass.

A compact viewport status shows the selected mode, effective quality, FPS, and GPU ON or GPU OFF.

It supports camera navigation, physical object sizes, contextual placement, grid controls, both bridge skies, Ride/Chase views, and qualitative spectral colors. Projected shapes and glows provide a schematic view. Current-frame image saving is available; VR and high-resolution Picture studio require WebGL.

## Model boundaries

The renderer integrates a Schwarzschild-inspired Cartesian light-bending model with explicit midpoint steps. Spin adds an illustrative transverse term and exact scalar Kerr horizon/ISCO radii. It is not a full Kerr geodesic or relativistic fluid solver. The math guide distinguishes exact theory from the implemented approximations.

The rendering world uses the default mass as a fixed reference, so changing mass changes horizon size without automatically moving the observer. Displayed distances use the primary hole's gravitational radius, `r_g = GM/c²`. The dark apparent shadow differs from the event horizon. Exterior contours follow the lensed capture silhouette. Coordinate inner/outer horizon curves appear only in the Interior geometry cutaway; Locator provides separate explanatory labels. None is light emitted by a horizon.

Ordinary binary motion is prescribed around the barycenter, with Roche-limited local discs and independently aligned jets. Merger presets add a prescribed inward motion and visual endpoint. An optional amplified quadrupolar grid cue illustrates gravitational waves; neither feature is a numerical-relativity calculation, a calibrated strain, or a prediction of remnant mass/spin. Black-hole–white-hole orbits and collisions are speculative comparisons, not solutions or annihilation claims. Combined lens fields, tidal debris, spacecraft trajectories, and plasma are approximate. Spectral views use false-color emission weights; composition options are visual templates, not resolved spectroscopy. Planetary diagnostics use mass and radius. Bodies use physical reference dimensions, and their backreaction is neglected. The continuous grid is a virtual luminous coordinate guide sampled along the same approximate bent light rays as the scene. It is anchored to the modeled holes, including both centers in a binary. Animated marks fall radially inward around an isolated nonspinning hole; spin adds an illustrative angular cue. Their motion weakens toward a stationary far field. This is a display convention, not an exact Kerr/binary spatial embedding or a physical spacetime fabric.

Ordinary black-hole cameras remain outside modeled capture surfaces. The separate **Interior geometry** cutaway permits a diagnostic interior view and singularity annotation; it does not simulate an infalling observer’s relativistic local frame or a visible material singularity. An infaller can receive outside light after crossing a future horizon, but cannot send a signal back out. Frequency shifts depend on observer motion and ray direction, not a universal freezing of the outside universe. The ideal Kerr inner horizon is unstable to perturbations. Motion uses accelerated visual time. Close inward views can become fully dark; look outward to inspect the surrounding sky. The Ellis bridge has no capture horizon and uses its separate metric and camera chart. Its numerical ray integration has finite step and boundary limits; the ship route and colored skies are demonstration choices.

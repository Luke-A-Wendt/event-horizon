## Object families

Black holes, stars, planets, ships, and astronauts can have children. Children inherit their parent’s motion and add their own local orbit. The placement hierarchy is black hole → star → planet → moon → spacecraft → astronaut. An equal-class pair is allowed, while comparable-mass triples and chains are rejected. Compact children with at most one tenth of their parent’s mass may form deeper, branching hierarchies. This is a composition rule, not a universal physical mass ordering. Paired bodies use prescribed barycentric circular motion; nested systems are not a numerical many-body stability solver.

Accretion discs and jets attach to black holes or stars; protostars and compact stars can accrete and launch outflows without an event horizon. Rings and asteroid belts attach to stars or planets and offer icy, rocky, and metallic appearances. These attachments are leaf nodes and cannot have satellites. Deleting a parent through the tree removes its whole branch. Reparenting rejects cycles and incompatible hosts. The logical scene supports up to 1,024 bodies and attachments, alongside the primary/binary black-hole rendering path. Additional compact bodies can form mass-separated hierarchies; children of at most one tenth their parent’s mass can share an orbital level, while comparable compact peers remain pair-limited. A bounded set of two to twelve relevant bodies, depending on scene size and GPU uniform capacity, is selected for detailed shading each frame; the catalog and object cameras retain the full hierarchy. Moons are a smaller class than planets, so a planet can have many moons. Pair restrictions apply to comparable compact peers and other equal-class objects.

Stars have editable class, color, radius, mass, angular velocity, spots, and corona. Classes include main-sequence stars, red dwarfs, protostars, red giants, supergiants, white dwarfs, neutron stars, and hypothetical Thorne–Żytkow objects. Stellar angular velocity Ω is in radians per second; a* is derived from an approximate moment of inertia and is not the same control. Mass and radius show the model’s compactness threshold. Crossing it changes the same object into a compact-horizon representation without losing its identity or children. This threshold is a visualization criterion, not a universal stability calculation: real collapse depends on pressure, equation of state, rotation, mass loss, and the full spacetime. **The point of no return** cycles radius through the threshold; its expanding half is explicitly a fictional reset. Near a black hole, a procedural tidal stream responds to distance, mass, and spin: zero spin removes its angular swirl. It illustrates stripped plasma rather than solving stellar magnetohydrodynamics. Planets offer rocky, ocean, gas, and ice appearances. Named worlds additionally offer reference surface maps, distinct atmospheric features, and approximate triaxial shapes for irregular bodies. Worlds with atmospheres have a thin, lit limb glow evaluated along the optical ray; Earth has a blue rim, Mars a restrained dusty haze, and airless bodies remain unlit at their edges. The Aurora disc style layers folded green, cyan and violet curtains with fine luminous rays. The starfield uses independent randomized layers with brightness and color-diversity controls.

Select **Track**, **Ride · 360°**, or **Free roam here** from an object's properties. Selecting NASA Meridian or the NASA Warp Explorer opens a persistent bottom flight panel. It shows **Auto** and **Drive** side by side. Drive takes the helm; Auto shows the editable playlist. Drive takes over at the live position and orientation. In a parking orbit, propulsion starts at zero and the vehicle stays in orbit until you apply power; steering alone changes its heading. During a transfer, Drive inherits the current velocity. Auto preserves the current motion until a route is chosen, cutting Shuttle thrust for an inertial coast. Lifetime clocks persist across both modes. The main **Camera** panel offers Orbit, Chase and Ride viewpoints. Warp changes fit the camera to the current bubble; at warp zero it frames the ship with clear margins around the controls. Corner steering buttons choose headings in 5° increments; holding a button repeats the steps. Manual Warp Explorer flight uses a Warp slider; the Shuttle uses forward acceleration. **Stop motion** slows the Warp Explorer, while **Stop** smoothly reduces Shuttle thrust to zero without removing its velocity.

**Opacity** is available in the properties of stars, planets, moons, probes, and spacecraft. Translucency reveals objects behind a surface without changing its mass or physical dimensions. Geometry mode uses opaque gray bodies.

Scene-card images are cached screenshots of the live High view, shared across desktop and mobile. Most cards show the opening view; selected travel scenes show a representative moment on the route, captured with the scene’s own renderer.

The normal 3D view uses physical reference sizes or explicitly labeled proxies for unmeasured bodies. Orbit map uses equal-size schematic symbols. Locator labels remain selectable for distant catalog bodies, with stable text placement and anchors from the displayed optical chart. GPU labels follow the approximate lens model; the CPU preview remains schematic.

The bridge is a distinct **Wormholes** scene family, not an orbitable body. Its default has a plain starfield on one side and a purple nebula on the other. Its coordinate sheet defaults to **Perpendicular**, normal to the traveler’s initial direction; **Parallel** is a separate choice. In **Shadow through the throat**, the black-hole grid stays in the flight plane. The **Math → Wormholes** notebook distinguishes the nontraversable Einstein–Rosen bridge from the Ellis traversal used on screen.

### Orbit map and geometric explanation

The collapsible **Instruments** panel on the left follows the camera. Track or ride a different object to see its instruments; selecting a playlist or editing another object does not change whose measurements you see.

- **Shuttle, ISS and astronaut** share a crew display. Speed and **log₁₀(v/c)** use coordinate distance traveled per second of distant reference time: −1 at 0.1c, approaching zero near light speed. **Felt** acceleration is what an onboard accelerometer would measure. **Clock loss rate** shows how much time the onboard clock loses relative to the distant clock per onboard second. Motion shows local seconds minus onboard seconds; Gravity shows distant seconds minus local seconds. The clock model still uses local speed for its motion factor. Both losses are per onboard second and add to the total before rounding. **Clock lag** is the accumulated difference between the two clocks.
- **NASA Warp Explorer** shows effective travel speed, its multiple of light speed and the warp factor. The simulated bubble protects the occupants, so ship travel speed does not generate crew acceleration or clock warnings.
- **Body survey** measures from the camera to the observed object. Center distance is distinct from the gap to a modeled surface or horizon. Where the model supplies them, rotation about the body’s own axis, orbital motion about its parent and the angular velocity of a rotating horizon are labeled separately. A horizon gap is a radial coordinate difference, not the distance a ruler would measure along curved space.

The **Map** toolbar button opens a top-down hierarchy rooted at **Space**. Each level shows the selected parent and its direct children. Select a parent and choose **Add child** to extend its branch, or select a child and choose **Remove** to remove its branch. **Add to playlist** queues a destination without leaving this map. The **Flight playlist** combines editable stops and their connections in one itinerary. Select a stop name for its physical properties, or select the travel time beneath it for onboard and distant clocks, peak speed and distance in light-travel units. Transfer estimates exclude time spent circling at a stop. Distances can use a logarithmic or linear radial scale; equal-size body symbols keep the Sun, planets, or a planet’s moons readable together. Select a body to edit its properties, track or ride it, or center a map on its children. Double-click or double-tap a body to make it the map scope; the breadcrumb path moves back outward through its ancestors. **Explore children** also centers the map on a body’s children. The orbit-radius slider follows the map’s radial scale and updates the derived Keplerian period and speed; it changes the actual orbit, while the map’s display-scale controls do not resize the physical scene. A potential/clock-deficit inset has an explicit amplification control; it is not an exact metric embedding. The guide explains the distinction between potential depth, tidal curvature, and local free fall. Massive bodies have timelike four-velocity norm −c², not a local spatial speed of c.

A circular orbit can have constant speed while its velocity direction changes in central-body coordinates. A freely falling body has zero proper acceleration and parallel-transports its four-velocity along a spacetime geodesic. A local inertial frame makes the path straight to first order in a small neighborhood; no single flat diagram does this for the whole orbit. The mathematics guide includes the geodesic equation, weak-field metric, tidal acceleration, curvature invariant, and the map’s scale transformation.

### Save and load scenes

Open **Scenes → Save scene** to download a human-readable `.yaml` file. **Load scene** opens a saved file from your device. Objects are nested beneath their parents, alongside editable properties; the file also stores the environment, display settings, simulation clock, and viewpoint. Loading opens the saved scene paused and retains the current GPU or CPU backend.

Scene files require an exact application-version match: **1.65.0**. A different version shows **Cannot load** and leaves the current scene unchanged. Scene files preserve navigation mode, route queues, loop settings, waypoints, power, pose and clocks. Loading pauses execution at the saved state; routes resume only after pressing Play. The reader accepts the generated YAML structure with ordinary scalar edits and validates object relationships, with a 4 MB file limit. Formatting and validation run in a local worker; browsers without worker support can handle only small scenes and files.

### Disc styles and observer studies

**The tide** follows Pelagia, a rocky ocean world with Earth’s mass and radius, at 65 GM/c² (2.76 AU) from a nonrotating black hole of 4.3 million solar masses. The planet orbits in 19.4 hours and spins in 11 hours. The tracking camera keeps a patch of rocky coastline in view as water advances and retreats. The two tidal highs follow the hole: one faces it and the other faces away. [See the same coast at 0, 22 and 44 hours](dist/assets/pelagia-shoreline-study.webp). The orbit is about 2.36 times the classical fluid Roche estimate. A more distant star supplies daylight. Liquid water is an assumption, not a climate prediction.

The equilibrium forcing is U/g = R(M/m)(R/d)³ P₂(cos ψ). With assumed Earth-like Love numbers h₂ = 0.60 and k₂ = 0.30, rock moves by h₂ U/g and the sea equipotential by (1+k₂) U/g. Their difference gives a high-to-low sea-level range of **35.2 km relative to the ground**; the rock itself has a 30.2 km tidal range. The ocean inventory is conserved to first order over wet cells. For visibility, only the offshore crests more than 7 km above the reference datum are magnified, with up to **65× vertical relief**. Shorelines, dry land and the reported depths and ranges use the physical model. This is an evolving equilibrium tide; it does not solve currents, breaking waves, ocean loading, a fully relativistic tidal tensor, or long-term evolution of the planet.

Model references: [IERS Conventions, §6.3 (relative equilibrium response)](https://amsat-bda.org/files/IERS_Conventions_2003.pdf), [NOAA, solid-Earth tide](https://www.ospo.noaa.gov/Products/documents/J2_handbook_v1-4_no_rev.pdf), [classical Roche estimate and its assumptions, §1.4](https://www.astro.umd.edu/sites/default/files/theses/people/2023_Marohnic.pdf).

**Physical** disc color retains the selected material’s thermal-inspired palette; **Artistic** allows freely chosen colors without claiming a temperature measurement. Jets offer steady, knotted, and helical emissivity with independent color, spread, length, and brightness. **Radiant** adds brilliant blue-white plasma; **Ribbons** produces separated electric-blue currents. These procedural appearances use the lensed rays and scale their detail with render quality. They are visual emissivity choices, not calibrated chemical or fluid simulations.

**Falling into blue fire** follows an astronaut released from rest relative to a locally nonrotating observer at 180 gravitational radii. On this equatorial free-fall trajectory both local speed and the distant-to-onboard clock ratio increase, while the onboard accelerometer reads zero. Frame dragging changes the astronaut’s azimuth without applying thrust. Playback stretches the final approach so that the rapid change in the view can be followed; its duration is independent of the physical journey. At the default 15 m exterior gap the worldline sample and both clocks freeze; only the camera continues around the astronaut. This is a freeze-frame, not an astronaut braking to hover. Tidal injury, collisions with disc matter and radiation are not modeled. **Looking out from the edge** holds the astronaut at constant radius using hypothetical support. Around a rotating hole, that lookout moves in azimuth with frame dragging. Drag to take over the view; reset restores the guide.

Luminous probes have individual logarithmic brightness controls: `L / L0 = 10^b`, with `b` from 0 to 8. Changing luminosity changes emission and its visible halo, not physical dimensions. The multiplier is relative, not a calibrated wattage, and does not model radiation pressure or heating.

### White holes and bridge traversal

The white-hole preset reverses the radial reference-grid flow. In the ideal nonrotating spacetime, this corresponds to outgoing rather than ingoing Painlevé–Gullstrand coordinates. The exterior still bends light like a positive-mass Schwarzschild geometry. A white hole is not a negative-mass repeller, and its name does not imply a white thermal surface. The pale emission in this scene is an illustrative boundary source. White holes remain hypothetical; any added spin swirl or plasma is a schematic visual control.

The **Einstein–Rosen bridge traversal study** deliberately distinguishes two models: the Schwarzschild Einstein–Rosen bridge is **not traversable**, while the live crossing uses an ideal **Ellis wormhole**, with no event horizon. The guide explains both and provides a causal diagram plus an equatorial embedding. In proper radial distance `ell`, the Ellis metric is `ds² = -c²dt² + d ell² + (ell²+b²)dΩ²`. ### Persistent object clocks

When the camera follows an astronaut, the ISS, or a Garden Station, that subject’s clock panel sits at the top of the instruments. Looking around does not change which clock is measured. **Proper time** is elapsed onboard time, τ. **Clock lag** is the accumulated difference t − τ relative to the distant reference clock. **Clock loss rate** is dt/dτ − 1, expressed as seconds (or fractions of a second) lost **per onboard second**. For example, at dt/dτ = 2, one onboard second passes while two distant seconds pass: the clock loses 1 s/s. The rotating station’s clock is at the hub.

Screen time is a separate quantity. A guided leg can show days in seconds; slowing playback changes when the viewer reaches a given point, not the clocks at that point. Pausing holds the readings. Changing the camera target selects that subject’s clock without restarting it. At the cutoff in **Falling into blue fire** the astronaut and both clocks freeze, even though the helmet camera continues to turn. The resulting clock ratio compares the traveler to the distant time coordinate; it does not measure a change caused directly by felt acceleration. Guided routes and free fall integrate their own physical intervals.

Scene selection immediately clears the viewport to empty space. Graphics prepare in the background, and only the new scene’s first completed frame is revealed. The scene title and description stay visible during loading. Instruments appear with the first completed frame for the current view. A stalled grid or camera transition brings back the loading screen, with scene selection still available. A retry action appears when rendering fails. Browsers without worker WebGL use a main-thread GPU fallback, so driver stalls cannot be fully isolated on every device.

Geometry mode renders shaded gray stars and planets along the same optical rays as the scene, including lensing and foreground occlusion. The surrounding grid remains a coordinate diagnostic within the approximate optical model. The compact-star orbital study slows its simulation clock to show a circuit in roughly 30 seconds, without changing the modeled orbit size or mass.

### Paused views

Pausing keeps the selected live quality and camera controls active. Use **Picture studio** for high-resolution image rendering.

### CPU preview when WebGL is unavailable

The site attempts WebGL first. If WebGL cannot start, it offers a low-fidelity CPU preview or a retry of GPU rendering. Adaptive quality handles low GPU frame rates within the WebGL renderer.

The CPU preview uses Canvas 2D with independent Low, Medium, High, and Ultra profiles. Their pixel ceilings are 224×148, 320×212, 448×296, and 720×472; grid, shadow, surface, and visible-body budgets scale with each level. The 64×42 startup profile gives way to larger live frames as draws complete. Adaptive can trade cadence for resolution under load, using the same 15 and 30 FPS targets as the GPU renderer. All scene types remain available as schematic previews. A compact viewport status shows the selected mode, effective quality, FPS, and GPU ON or GPU OFF.

It supports camera navigation, physical object sizes, contextual placement, grid controls, both bridge skies, Ride/Chase views, and qualitative spectral colors. Projected shapes and glows provide a schematic view. Current-frame image saving is available; VR and high-resolution Picture studio require WebGL.

## Model boundaries

The renderer integrates a Schwarzschild-inspired Cartesian light-bending model with explicit midpoint steps. Spin adds an illustrative transverse term and exact scalar Kerr horizon/ISCO radii. It is not a full Kerr geodesic or relativistic fluid solver. The math guide distinguishes exact theory from the implemented approximations.

The rendering world uses the default mass as a fixed reference, so changing mass changes horizon size without automatically moving the observer. Displayed distances use the primary hole's gravitational radius, `r_g = GM/c²`. The dark apparent shadow differs from the event horizon. Exterior contours follow the lensed capture silhouette. Coordinate inner/outer horizon curves appear only in the Interior geometry cutaway; Locator provides separate explanatory labels. None is light emitted by a horizon.

Ordinary binary motion is prescribed around the barycenter, with Roche-limited local discs and independently aligned jets. Merger presets add a prescribed inward motion and visual endpoint. An optional amplified quadrupolar grid cue illustrates gravitational waves; neither feature is a numerical-relativity calculation, a calibrated strain, or a prediction of remnant mass/spin. Black-hole–white-hole orbits and collisions are speculative comparisons, not solutions or annihilation claims. Combined lens fields, tidal debris, spacecraft trajectories, and plasma are approximate. Spectral views use false-color emission weights; composition options are visual templates, not resolved spectroscopy. Planetary diagnostics use mass and radius. Bodies use physical reference dimensions, and their backreaction is neglected. The continuous grid is a virtual luminous coordinate guide sampled along the same approximate bent light rays as the scene. It is anchored to the modeled holes, including both centers in a binary. Animated marks fall radially inward around an isolated nonspinning hole; spin adds an illustrative angular cue. Their motion weakens toward a stationary far field. This is a display convention, not an exact Kerr/binary spatial embedding or a physical spacetime fabric.

Ordinary black-hole cameras remain outside modeled capture surfaces. The separate **Interior geometry** cutaway permits a diagnostic interior view and singularity annotation; it does not simulate an infalling observer’s relativistic local frame or a visible material singularity. An infaller can receive outside light after crossing a future horizon, but cannot send a signal back out. Frequency shifts depend on observer motion and ray direction, not a universal freezing of the outside universe. The ideal Kerr inner horizon is unstable to perturbations. Motion uses accelerated visual time. Close inward views can become fully dark; look outward to inspect the surrounding sky. The Ellis bridge has no capture horizon and uses its separate metric and camera chart. Its numerical ray integration has finite step and boundary limits; the ship route and colored skies are demonstration choices.


**Driving in open space** parks Janus, the shuttle, Warp Explorer, Meridian and Enterprise in order of increasing hull size and distance from the starting camera. Meridian uses its station-docking scale. Click or tap a ship or its label to inspect it. Double-click or double-tap the same ship to take its helm. Choose **Camera → View all vehicles** to return to the lineup.
